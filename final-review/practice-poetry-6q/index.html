Question 1

Hard · Central idea

At first I hid the mended cup behind the plates whose rims had never met the floor. But morning found the golden seam and laid a narrow sunrise on the kitchen door. Now every guest receives that cup from me: its break is where the careful hands can be.

Which choice best states the main idea of the text?

  1. A. The speaker regrets that a repaired object can no longer appear flawless to visitors.
  2. B. The speaker concludes that replacing damaged objects is usually less costly than repairing them.
  3. C. The speaker comes to value a visible repair as evidence of care and endurance rather than as a defect.
  4. D. The speaker uses a neighbor’s repair technique to make an ordinary cup more decorative.

Question 2

Hard · Evidence

No milepost keeps its number in this rain; the road unthreads itself beyond the hill. I hear no promise from the darkened plain, yet set my soaked and stubborn boots there still. Behind me, every certain lamp grows small; ahead, the weather will not speak at all.

Which quotation from the text best supports the claim that the speaker persists despite uncertainty?

  1. A. “the road unthreads itself beyond the hill. / I hear no promise . . . / yet set my . . . boots there still.”
  2. B. “No milepost keeps its number in this rain”
  3. C. “Behind me, every certain lamp grows small”
  4. D. “ahead, the weather will not speak at all”

Question 3

Hard · Inference

The clock performs its little metal task; the kettle answers, punctual and thin. Your chair keeps facing mine across the glass, as though the room had not been altered within. Nothing has moved. The ordinary air touches each object—and finds you nowhere.

Which choice most logically completes the text’s meaning?

  1. A. The speaker suspects that the clock stopped when the other person left.
  2. B. The speaker expects the other person to return before the kettle sounds again.
  3. C. The speaker avoids noticing familiar objects because they are physically damaged.
  4. D. The speaker experiences an unchanged room as transformed by another person’s absence.

Question 4

Hard · Phrase function

Across the bay, the harbor’s lamps appear— a necklace measured out on threads of night— and though no sailor’s call can travel here, each patient bead makes distance briefly bright.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined phrase in the text as a whole?

  1. A. It suggests that the harbor lights prevent sailors from navigating safely.
  2. B. It supplies a visual comparison that makes the harbor’s distance seem delicate but measurable.
  3. C. It reveals that the speaker is observing the bay from a boat inside the harbor.
  4. D. It emphasizes that the harbor is much closer to the speaker than its lights initially suggest.

Question 5

Hard · Overall structure

I called the garden slow: the buds held tight, the soil gave back no answer to my spade. Then, waiting where the fence received the light, I watched one shadow shorten as it strayed. By dusk a green point lifted through the ground; I called the garden quiet, not unsound.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

  1. A. The speaker reports a disagreement, supplies evidence for both sides, and refuses to choose between them.
  2. B. The speaker abandons an observation, recalls a past success, and predicts a future failure.
  3. C. The speaker begins with an impatient judgment, attends to gradual changes, and revises that judgment.
  4. D. The speaker praises a setting, identifies an unexpected danger, and returns to the original praise unchanged.

Question 6

Hard · Cross-text

Text 1

A critic argues that the bell recurring in a long poem is chiefly decorative. Because the same sound appears at the end of several stanzas, the critic treats it as a musical refrain that organizes the poem without changing its meaning.

Text 2

Another critic notes that the bell first marks a departure, later interrupts a celebration, and finally accompanies the speaker’s return. The sound repeats, but the events surrounding it alter what the bell can suggest.

Based on the texts, how would the critic in Text 2 most likely respond to the interpretation in Text 1?

  1. A. The bell is not a refrain because it appears only once in the poem.
  2. B. The poem’s historical setting is inaccurate, so the bell cannot organize the stanzas.
  3. C. The bell has exactly the same meaning in each scene, although its volume changes.
  4. D. The bell may organize the poem, but its changing contexts also develop its significance.
