Text Structure and Purpose: Read the Author’s Moves

Joy · Class 16 · Craft and Structure

Today’s target

These questions are not satisfied by a correct summary. You must identify why a sentence appears, how the whole text moves, or what rhetorical job the writer is performing.

By the end of class, you should be able to use three routines without notes:

  1. Content-to-Function Map — turn “what this sentence says” into “what this sentence does here.”
  2. Structure Arrow — compress the text into ordered moves joined by accurate arrows.
  3. Purpose = Topic + Job + Scope — name both the subject and the writer’s exact action without becoming too broad or too narrow.

5-minute bridge: evidence words still control the answer

What happened? A study found that a restored stream held more insects after shade trees matured.

Why might the writer include that result? It could support a restoration claim, illustrate a mechanism, qualify an earlier prediction, or motivate another test. The surrounding sentences decide the job.

Keep the Class 15 habit: point to the shortest decisive words. Today, connect those words to a rhetorical role.

Takeaway 1 — Content-to-Function Map

Recognition cue: the question asks for the function or purpose of an underlined sentence, phrase, detail, example, or quotation.

  1. Open the window: read the underlined part plus at least one sentence before and after it.
  2. Write the content note: reduce the underlined part to five to eight words.
  3. Link it: complete “This detail helps the writer ___ by ___.”
  4. Lock the function verb: choose the exact job—illustrates, supports, qualifies, contrasts, defines, explains, introduces, or concludes.

content note + relationship to nearby claim = function

Trap logic: a choice may accurately repeat the underlined content but assign the wrong job. Also reject a function verb that is too strong: evidence can support a claim without proving it.

Difficult demonstration

Restoration teams had assumed that scattering native seeds across a mined slope would be enough to rebuild the former plant community. Five years later, plots with seeds but no restored soil fungi contained many seedlings yet few mature native plants. The result suggests that visible early growth can overstate long-term recovery when belowground partnerships are missing.

Content note: seedlings appeared, but few plants matured.

Before/after link: the first sentence states an assumption; the last sentence draws a warning from the result.

Function: the underlined sentence provides evidence that qualifies the assumption and supports the warning about early growth.

Why “describes the restored community” is weak: it tells what the sentence says but not why the writer uses it.

Moderate

Environmental agencies sometimes estimate airborne nitrogen by recording color changes in lichens. Yet lichen color can also respond to moisture and shade. In a transplant experiment, genetically similar lichens exposed to the same nitrogen level developed different colors when placed under open sky and beneath a dense canopy. The researchers therefore caution that color alone is an unreliable measure of nitrogen exposure.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  1. It defines the chemical process that causes lichens to absorb nitrogen.
  2. It presents a result confirming that lichen color depends only on nitrogen exposure.
  3. It provides evidence supporting the warning that factors other than nitrogen can affect lichen color.
  4. It proposes a new method for measuring moisture under forest canopies.

Content note: same nitrogen + different light conditions → different colors.

Nearby claim: color alone is unreliable because other conditions affect it.

Function sentence: “This result helps the writer ___ the warning by ___.”

Takeaway 2 — Structure Arrow

Recognition cue: the question asks for the text’s overall structure or how the discussion is organized.

  1. Cut at turn points: mark transitions, a change of subject, a new study, a result, or a limitation.
  2. Verb each move: write one action verb per section: introduces, reports, compares, challenges, explains, narrows, or concludes.
  3. Draw the arrows: show the relationship—support, contrast, cause, revision, example, or qualification.
  4. Match every move: the answer must preserve the order, direction, and importance of the moves.

Move 1 → relationship → Move 2 → relationship → Move 3

Trap logic: a list of topics is not a structure. Reject answers that reverse the order, invent a disagreement, turn a limitation into a rejection, or promote a minor detail into a main move.

Difficult demonstration

Historians once treated the nearly identical wording in workers’ petitions from three textile towns as evidence that a national organization had written them. Newly catalogued wage ledgers, however, show that the petitions appeared first in the town where pay cuts began earliest. Letters exchanged among local committees then carried the phrasing to the other towns. The common language thus reflects local coordination that spread outward rather than a message imposed from a national office.

Move 1: presents an earlier explanation.

Move 2: introduces chronological and documentary evidence.

Move 3: replaces the top-down explanation with a local-to-regional account.

Structure Arrow: earlier interpretation → new evidence → revised origin-and-spread explanation.

Question 3 · Guided application

Difficult

Biologists predicted that a fungus linking the roots of neighboring beech trees would move carbon mainly from mature trees to shaded saplings. Tracer experiments did detect carbon moving through the fungal network, but most of it remained in fungal tissue rather than entering the saplings. The researchers propose that the fungus may retain the carbon for its own growth, a possibility that complicates the original account of direct tree-to-tree support.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

  1. It describes two competing experiments and argues that neither can detect carbon movement.
  2. It introduces a fungal process, lists its benefits, and recommends expanding it to other forests.
  3. It reports a prediction and then presents evidence that fully confirms the predicted destination of the carbon.
  4. It presents a prediction, reports a partly unexpected result, and offers an explanation that complicates the prediction.

Move 1: predicted destination of carbon.

Move 2: movement detected, but most carbon stopped elsewhere.

Move 3: a fungus-centered explanation complicates the original account.

Takeaway 3 — Purpose = Topic + Job + Scope

Recognition cue: the question asks for the main or overall purpose of the text.

  1. Topic: name the repeated subject in a few words.
  2. Job: use the Structure Arrow to identify what the writer does with that topic.
  3. Scope: preserve the text’s boundary—one study, a comparison, a caution, a proposal, or a qualified claim.
  4. Build the purpose: “The writer [job] [topic] in order to [larger point].”

purpose = topic + rhetorical job + correct boundary

Trap logic: reject a true detail that covers only one sentence, a summary that omits the writer’s job, and a broad claim that goes beyond the text. An accurate purpose usually contains both a content noun and an action verb.

Difficult demonstration

Household inventories from an eighteenth-century port often list imported tea sets under the name of the male household head. Receipts and repair notes, however, show that women in those households selected designs, arranged credit, and decided which damaged pieces were worth preserving. The documents do not erase legal limits on women’s ownership, but they reveal forms of commercial decision-making that the inventories alone conceal.

Topic: what two record types reveal about women’s role in household purchases.

Job: contrasts a narrow official record with other documents that expose hidden agency.

Scope: expands the evidence without claiming that legal inequality disappeared.

Purpose: to show how additional records complicate an inventory-based view of women’s commercial role.

Question 5 · Guided application

Difficult

A set of unsigned notes in the margins of a naturalist’s field book was once attributed to the book’s owner. Close inspection shows that the notes were written in three inks and use two distinct systems of abbreviation. Shipping records also place the owner abroad when several dated comments were added. Together, these details indicate that assistants continued annotating the book, making it a shared working record rather than a single observer’s private journal.

Which choice best states the main purpose of the text?

  1. To criticize the naturalist for failing to identify the assistants who organized the shipping records
  2. To describe the chemical process used to determine the exact age of three inks
  3. To explain why evidence supports reinterpreting the field book as a collaborative document
  4. To argue that unsigned notes are generally more reliable than signed observations

Topic: authorship and use of the field book.

Job: presents several clues and draws a reinterpretation.

Scope: this book; collaborative use—not all unsigned notes.

Because a lasting kelp forest must survive across seasons and produce new growth, researchers recommend tracking adult persistence and reproduction in addition to the first season’s plant count.

  3. To argue that winter storms make all coastal restoration projects ineffective

Difficult

Researchers investigating ancient food preparation found traces of fermented grain inside large ceramic vessels. Because fermentation can occur accidentally after grain becomes wet, the residue alone could not establish deliberate brewing. The vessels’ narrow openings and repeated interior heating marks, however, match features that help control temperature during fermentation. The combination of residue and vessel design makes intentional brewing more plausible.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  1. It defines fermentation by listing the chemical reactions that occur inside grain.
  2. It introduces evidence showing that the grain residue was produced by accidental moisture.
  3. It describes damage that prevents researchers from determining how the vessels were used.
  4. It adds design evidence that strengthens an interpretation the residue alone cannot establish.

Drone photographs later showed that several bands crossed small ridges rather than following the steepest route. Samples then revealed windblown mineral dust concentrated in shallow surface grooves. The researchers conclude that meltwater deepened some grooves, but wind first deposited the dark material that made the bands visible.

Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?

  1. It outlines two mapping technologies and argues that neither can identify glacier features accurately.
  2. It presents a disagreement between research teams and explains why the teams reached identical conclusions.
  3. It introduces an initial interpretation, presents evidence against its complete version, and offers a combined explanation.
  4. It describes how mineral dust melts a glacier and proposes removing the dust from mountain ridges.

The pattern suggests that the pauses are a deliberate device for marking shifts in the speaker’s stance.

Question 10

Difficult

Standardized stone weights found in a desert market suggest that merchants used shared units when pricing metal and grain. Ledger fragments likewise record weight-based prices for goods sold in bulk, indicating that the stones were not merely ceremonial objects. Capacity marks scratched inside storage jars vary considerably, though, even among jars from the same stall. The contrast indicates that standardization may have governed some transactions while everyday measures for stored goods remained locally flexible.

Which choice best describes the function of the underlined sentence in the text as a whole?

  1. It introduces contrasting evidence that limits how broadly the stone weights can support a claim about standardization.
  2. It proves that merchants rejected standardized units in every kind of transaction.
  3. It explains how archaeologists calculated the exact volume of each storage jar.
  4. It provides an example of a shared unit used to price metal and grain.
