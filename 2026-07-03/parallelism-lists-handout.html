Joy SAT Lesson 07: Parallelism and Lists

0703 Class 7

Today's Goal

Parallelism means that equal ideas should use matching grammar. Today we will use that idea for lists, paired connectors, and comparisons.

Fast check: Find the connector, bracket the equal parts, compare their grammar shape, then choose the answer that keeps the shape consistent.

1. Parallelism Means Matching Shapes

When two or more ideas do the same job in a sentence, their grammar should match. A list of actions should use matching action forms; a list of things should use matching noun forms; a list of descriptions should use matching adjectives.

Recognition cue: Look for and, or, but, commas in a list, or repeated words such as to, by, or for. Then ask: are the items the same kind of phrase?
Example: The researcher sorted the samples, labeled the jars, and recorded the results.
Common mistake: The researcher sorted the samples, labeled the jars, and recording the results.
Question 1
Easy

To prepare the museum's textile collection for a traveling exhibition, interns practiced photographing fragile fabrics, entering catalog data, and ______ conservation notes.

A. to write
B. writing
C. wrote
D. they write

2. Three-Item Lists

In a three-item list, all three items should be built the same way: noun + noun + noun, adjective + adjective + adjective, gerund + gerund + gerund, or infinitive + infinitive + infinitive.

Recognition cue: If a sentence has item 1, item 2, and item 3, compare the last item to the first two. SAT questions often hide the error in the final item.
Example: The workshop teaches students to plan a budget, to track expenses, and to compare prices.
Common mistake: The workshop teaches students to plan a budget, to track expenses, and comparing prices.
Question 2
Easy

A. bike lanes that are protected
B. protecting bike lanes
C. protected bike lanes
D. to protect bike lanes

3. Paired Connectors

Some connector pairs require the same grammar after both sides: both ... and, either ... or, neither ... nor, not only ... but also, rather than, from ... to, between ... and, as well as.

Recognition cue: Bracket the words after each connector. The two bracketed parts should match in form and meaning.
Example: The archive is useful both for preserving local history and for helping students conduct research.
Common mistake: The archive is useful both for preserving local history and students conduct research.

Medium

A. replacing
B. replaced
C. replace
D. the replacement of

4. Comparisons Must Compare the Same Kind of Thing

A comparison should compare people to people, costs to costs, methods to methods, or one object's feature to another object's feature. The grammar after than, as ... as, like, unlike, similar to, and different from must make the comparison logical.

Recognition cue: Ask: what exactly is being compared? If the sentence compares a feature, the other side should be the same feature or a clear substitute such as that of or those of.
Example: The new battery's lifespan is longer than that of the previous model.
Common mistake: The new battery's lifespan is longer than the previous model.

Medium

C. ten years ago was solar energy

Question 8
Easy

C. as accurately than
D. most accurate as

One-Minute Checklist

  1. Find the connector or list punctuation.
  2. Bracket the equal ideas.
  3. Name each shape: noun phrase, adjective, gerund, infinitive, finite verb, or clause.
  4. For comparisons, ask what is being compared to what.
  5. Pick the answer that keeps both grammar and meaning parallel.
