Big Idea

Form, Structure, and Sense questions are grammar logic questions. The answer must fit the sentence structure and the meaning of the surrounding text.

These questions usually look short, but they test whether the sentence is built correctly. First find the sentence core, then decide what form the blank needs.

1. Finite Verb or Nonfinite Verb?

A finite verb can be the main verb of a sentence or clause. It shows tense or agrees with a subject: measures, left, includes, was.

A nonfinite verb form cannot be the main verb by itself. It usually explains purpose, adds description, or works inside a phrase: to join, joining, known, having joined.

finite: subject + real verb

nonfinite: to + verb / verb-ing / past participle phrase

Demonstration: Ellen left her job to join the training program.
left is the main verb. to join is not a second main verb; it explains why she left.
Sentence NeedCorrect FormExample
The sentence has no main verb yet.Finite verbOne species includes two ecotypes.
The sentence already has a main verb and needs a purpose.InfinitiveShe left the lab to join a new program.
The sentence already has a main verb and adds a result.-ing phraseThe discovery changed the timeline, pushing the date earlier.
  • If the sentence has no real verb, choose a finite verb.
  • If the sentence already has a real verb, the blank may need a nonfinite phrase.
  • Do not add two finite verbs to one clause unless they are correctly joined.

2. Verb Tense: Follow The Time Clues

Verb tense shows when an action happens. SAT tense questions usually include a time clue nearby. Do not choose the most complicated tense; choose the tense that matches the timeline.

Time MeaningCommon ClueVerb TenseExample
General fact or current functionusually, generally, today, a tool's functionSimple presentThe index measures quality of life.
Completed historical eventin 1881, in the 1970s, from 1440 to 1473Simple pastFaure redesigned the battery in 1881.
Earlier of two past actionsby the time, earlier, already, beforePast perfectPlanté had invented the battery fifteen years earlier.
Demonstration: Faure redesigned the battery in 1881. Planté had invented the original battery fifteen years earlier.
The invention happened first, so it uses had invented.
  • Use simple present for general facts or current functions.
  • Use simple past for completed historical events.
  • Use past perfect for an earlier past action before a later past action.

3. Subject-Verb Agreement

A verb must match its real subject. SAT often hides the subject behind names, appositives, prepositional phrases, and long descriptions.

Demonstration: The equation, named for its creator, remains important.
Subject = equation. The phrase named for its creator is extra information. The verb must match equation, not creator.
TrapHow To Handle ItExample
Prepositional phraseIgnore the noun after of, in, for, etc.A cycle of lunar phases takes 29.5 days.
Appositive or descriptionCross out the extra phrase between commas.The market, one of many conferences, promotes the industry.
Each oneEach one is singular.Each one of the plays focuses on a historical figure.
  • Find the subject before looking at the choices.
  • Cross out interrupting phrases between subject and verb.
  • Do not let a nearby plural noun control the verb if it is not the subject.

4. Pronouns and Possessives

A pronoun replaces a noun. The noun it refers to is called the antecedent. A pronoun must match its antecedent in number: singular with singular, plural with plural.

A possessive form shows ownership or connection: its, their, novel's, photographer's.

Demonstration: Emily Dickinson copied more than 800 of her poems into homemade booklets.
The pronoun should refer to poems, which is plural, so the correct pronoun is them, not it.
FormMeaningExample
itsbelonging to itThe animal nurses its young.
it'sit is / it hasIt's unusual for a mammal to lay eggs.
theirbelonging to themThe poems have their own structure.
they'rethey areThey're arranged in panels.
  • Find the antecedent first.
  • Check singular vs plural.
  • Do not confuse possessives with contractions.

5. Modifier Placement

A modifier is a word, phrase, or clause that describes another word. The word being described is the target of the modifier.

Modifier placement means putting the modifier close to the word it describes. On SAT questions, an opening modifier usually describes the first noun after the comma.

Correct: Far from being modern inventions, drinking straws were used more than 5,000 years ago.
The modifier Far from being modern inventions describes drinking straws.
Wrong: Far from being modern inventions, Sumerians used drinking straws more than 5,000 years ago.
This accidentally says Sumerians were not modern inventions. That is illogical.
ModifierQuestion To AskCorrect Target
Far from being modern inventionsWhat things are inventions?drinking straws
A coming-of-age story, a social meditation, and a sober gaze into colonialismWhat is a story / meditation / gaze?the novel
Known for its unusual structureWhat is known for that structure?the poem / book / artwork
  • Find the modifier: usually the phrase before the comma.
  • Ask what noun the modifier logically describes.
  • Choose the answer that places that noun immediately after the comma.
  • If the noun after the comma is a person, place, or study but the modifier describes an object or artwork, the choice is probably wrong.
