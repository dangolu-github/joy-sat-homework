Central Ideas and Details

Joy SAT Reading & Writing • Class 11 • July 13, 2026

Today’s target

Read a short passage and decide exactly what the question wants:

  • Central idea: the one statement that covers the passage as a whole.
  • Detail: the one statement that matches a specific fact, reason, action, or result in the text.

The five-step reading route

  1. Name the task: whole passage or specific detail?
  2. Name the topic: who or what appears throughout the text?
  3. Add the “so what?”: what is the passage saying about that topic?
  4. Predict briefly: write a few words before reading the choices.
  5. Match, then eliminate: choose the paraphrase supported by the text.
TOPIC + SO WHAT? = CENTRAL IDEA

1. Name the task before solving

A central-idea question asks about the whole passage. A detail question points you toward one specific part.

Recognition cue
“Which choice best states the main idea?” means whole passage. “According to the text, why/what/how...?” means locate and match a detail.
Example
A passage defines a coastal fog net, explains how it collects droplets, and describes its use in dry communities. A central-idea answer must include the fog net and its useful function. A question asking what the mesh collects requires only the detail water droplets.

2. Build a whole-passage central idea

First identify the repeated subject. Then decide what all the sentences collectively show about it. A correct central idea usually combines information from more than one sentence.

Recognition cue
Think in sentence jobs: introduces topic → explains method/change/problem → gives result or importance. Your answer must fit that full movement.
Too narrow
“The map contains blue symbols.” This may be true, but it covers one detail only.
Whole passage
“A newly decoded map reveals how merchants organized regional trade.” This includes the topic and why the map matters.

3. Control scope and reject trap answers

The correct answer stays inside the text’s boundaries. It is broad enough to cover the passage but not broader than the passage.

Too narrow
Repeats one example or minor fact.
Too broad
Makes a claim about a whole field, era, or group when the passage discusses one case.
Twisted or contradicted
Uses familiar words but reverses or changes the relationship.
New or extreme
Adds an unsupported cause, judgment, “only,” “always,” or “most important.”
Example
If a passage reports that one community reduced household waste by sharing repair tools, “all cities should ban new products” is too broad and unsupported—even though it sounds related.

Question 6 — SAT-Style Practice

Moderate

Historian Amara Okafor examined market records from one West African port between 1880 and 1900. Imported cloth appears frequently in the records, but locally woven cloth continued to command higher prices in several neighborhoods. Okafor argues that buyers valued local patterns and production knowledge even as imported goods became more available.

A. Imported cloth was unavailable in West African ports before 1900.
B. Records from one port suggest that local textiles retained value despite increased access to imported cloth.
C. Locally woven cloth was the most expensive product sold in every neighborhood.
D. Buyers rejected all cloth produced outside their own neighborhoods.

4. Locate a detail and match the paraphrase

For a detail question, use the wording in the question as a map. Return to the sentence containing that idea and read one sentence before or after when needed. The correct answer may use different words, but it must keep the same meaning.

Recognition cue
Underline the target: a person, object, time, action, reason, comparison, or result. Then finish this sentence: “The text says ______.”
Same words, wrong meaning
The choice repeats “migration” but names the wrong cause.
Different words, same meaning
moved to cooler water accurately paraphrases shifted toward lower-temperature zones.

Question 8 — SAT-Style Practice

Difficult

Literary scholar Mateo Ruiz compared several handwritten drafts of a short story by Clarice Lispector. In the earliest draft, the final scene explains the protagonist’s decision directly. In later drafts, Lispector removed that explanation and replaced it with a brief description of an unopened window. Ruiz argues that the revision makes the ending less explicit and asks readers to infer the protagonist’s motives.

According to the text, what effect did Lispector’s revision have on the story’s ending?

A. It introduced a new protagonist.
B. It explained the protagonist’s decision more directly.
C. It removed the final scene entirely.
D. It made the protagonist’s motives less directly stated.

Exit routine

  1. Say main idea or detail.
  2. Give a prediction in ten words or fewer.
  3. For the three rejected choices, name the trap: narrow, broad, twisted, or new/extreme.
