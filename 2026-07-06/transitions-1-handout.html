Joy SAT Lesson 08: Transitions I

0706 Class 8

Today's Goal

  • Shorten the text into idea 1 and idea 2.
  • Find anchor words: dates, contrast clues, cause words, examples, numbers, or definitions.
  • Name the relationship before reading the answer choices.
  • Choose the transition with the same function.
Fast check: Say the relationship in plain English: same direction, contrast, cause/effect, example, time order, or restatement.

Transition Relationship Bank

  • Use these categories to name the relationship before checking the answer choices.
  • When two choices have the same function, both are usually suspicious unless one precisely matches the text.
Same direction
also, moreover, additionally, likewise, similarly
Example or specific detail
for example, for instance, specifically, in fact
Opposite direction
however, nevertheless, by contrast, instead, though
Cause and effect
therefore, as a result, consequently, for this reason, thus
Time or order
then, later, earlier, previously, subsequently
Restatement or summary
in other words, that is, in sum, in conclusion

1. The Four-Step Transition Method

  • Transition = the relationship between idea 1 and idea 2.
  • Step 1: shorten the sentence into two small ideas.
  • Step 2: find anchor words such as dates, contrast words, cause words, examples, or definitions.
  • Step 3: label the relationship before looking at the choices.
  • Step 4: choose the transition with that same function, then plug it back in.
Recognition cue: Shorten the text into two small ideas: idea before the blank and idea after the blank. Then label the relationship before checking the choices.

How It Looks In A Question

Mini demonstration 1: Contrast after new evidence
  • Idea 1: Earlier researchers counted only adult birds.
  • Idea 2: The later survey counted chicks too.
  • Relationship: The second method is different from the first.
  • Transition: However
  • Final sentence: Earlier researchers counted only adult birds. However, the later survey counted chicks too.
Mini demonstration 2: Sequence in a research process
  • Idea 1: The team measured plastic on the beach.
  • Idea 2: The team interviewed fishers.
  • Relationship: The second action is the next step.
  • Transition: Next
  • Final sentence: The team measured plastic on the beach. Next, the team interviewed fishers.
Common mistake: Choosing therefore would claim that the second survey happened as a result of the first survey, but the sentence is showing a difference.
Correct logic: The relationship is contrast, so however fits.
Takeaway: Do not start from the answer choices. Start from idea 1 + idea 2 + relationship.
Practice 1.1
Easy

A coastal research team first measured how much plastic washed onto a beach after storms. ______ the team interviewed local fishers to learn where the debris most often entered the water.

A. However,
B. Next,
C. By contrast,
D. In conclusion,
Practice 1.2
Hard

If school were operated on a year-round basis, student retention of material would be strengthened since it is much easier to forget material over a three-month break than over a two-week one. Material retention is especially vital for subjects like math and foreign language in which more advanced units will not make sense without a sound basis in the earlier material. ______ if students have more frequent breaks from the rigors of school, they are less likely to burn out.

A. However,
B. Consequently,
C. Additionally,
D. On the other hand,

2. Same Direction: Addition, Support, and Examples

  • Same-direction transitions mean idea 2 supports or continues idea 1.
  • Use addition words when idea 2 gives another supporting point.
  • Use example words when idea 2 gives one specific case of a general claim.
  • Use specific/detail words when idea 2 narrows or strengthens idea 1.
  • Do not use however/nevertheless when both ideas support the same claim.
Recognition cue: Look for a general claim followed by a specific case, or one supporting detail followed by another supporting detail.

How It Looks In A Question

Mini demonstration 1: General claim to example
  • Idea 1: Many libraries lend more than books.
  • Idea 2: One library lends sewing machines.
  • Relationship: Idea 2 is a concrete example of idea 1.
  • Transition: For example
  • Final sentence: Many libraries lend more than books. For example, one library lends sewing machines.
Mini demonstration 2: One benefit plus another benefit
  • Idea 1: Green roofs lower indoor temperatures.
  • Idea 2: Green roofs also absorb rainwater.
  • Relationship: Idea 2 adds another benefit in the same direction.
  • Transition: Moreover
  • Final sentence: Green roofs lower indoor temperatures. Moreover, they absorb rainwater.
Common mistake: However would suggest disagreement, but the second sentence supports the first sentence.
Correct logic: For example works because the second sentence gives one specific case.
Takeaway: Same direction = the second idea supports the first; choose the subtype: add, example, or specify.
Practice 2.1
Easy

Many seed banks protect crops that are important to regional food traditions. ______ one seed bank in New Mexico stores several varieties of blue corn used in local recipes.

A. For example,
B. Nevertheless,
C. Therefore,
D. Earlier,
Practice 2.2
Hard

Sunlight is different from the light that a person experiences from a fire or a light bulb. Sunlight is mostly UV light, which has a much shorter wavelength than the light we turn on when we use the bathroom in the middle of the night. ______ if the earth's protective ozone layer didn't filter out much of the UV light, humans would risk their health just by going about their everyday lives during daylight hours.

A. Meanwhile,
B. Nonetheless,
C. Indeed,
D. As a result,

3. Opposite Direction: Contrast and Concession

  • Opposite-direction transitions mean idea 2 turns away from idea 1.
  • A contrast can be direct: old belief vs. new evidence, one example vs. another example.
  • A concession can be softer: idea 1 is true, but idea 2 limits it.
  • Look for anchor words such as but, although, despite, fewer, different, rarely, instead, or less.
  • Do not use moreover/similarly when idea 2 creates surprise or limitation.
Recognition cue: Ask whether the second idea surprises you after the first idea. If yes, the relationship may be contrast or concession.

How It Looks In A Question

Mini demonstration 1: Appearance changes under a new condition
  • Idea 1: The ink looked black under normal light.
  • Idea 2: The ink looked red under infrared imaging.
  • Relationship: The two observations point in different directions.
  • Transition: However
  • Final sentence: The ink looked black under normal light. However, it looked red under infrared imaging.
Mini demonstration 2: Positive point plus limitation
  • Idea 1: The translation preserves the poem's rhyme.
  • Idea 2: Some readers find the wording less natural.
  • Relationship: Idea 2 limits the praise in idea 1.
  • Transition: That said
  • Final sentence: The translation preserves the poem's rhyme. That said, some readers find the wording less natural.
Common mistake: Moreover would suggest that the second sentence simply adds another expected point.
Correct logic: Nevertheless fits because the second idea is surprising despite the first idea.
Takeaway: Opposite direction = idea 2 turns away from idea 1, limits it, or gives an unexpected contrast.
Practice 3.1
Easy

The ancient ink looked black under normal light. ______ under infrared imaging, several lines appeared bright red.

A. However,
B. Likewise,
C. For this reason,
D. For example,
Practice 3.2
Hard

Easily one of the most distinctive painters associated with the surrealist movement of the early 20th century, Frida Kahlo ______ insisted adamantly throughout her career that she was by no means a surrealist herself.

A. consequently
B. moreover

D. nonetheless

4. Cause and Effect

  • Cause/effect transitions connect a reason to a result.
  • Result words point forward: therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, for this reason.
  • Reason words point backward: because, since.
  • Common SAT pattern: problem -> response, mechanism -> outcome, evidence -> conclusion.
  • Do not use for example when idea 2 happens because of idea 1.
Recognition cue: Look for a problem and a response, a condition and a result, or evidence that explains why something happened.

How It Looks In A Question

Mini demonstration 1: Problem to response
  • Idea 1: The river level dropped sharply.
  • Idea 2: Several ferry routes were suspended.
  • Relationship: The low water caused the suspension.
  • Transition: As a result
  • Final sentence: The river level dropped sharply. As a result, several ferry routes were suspended.
Mini demonstration 2: Risk to solution
  • Idea 1: Young mangrove trees can be damaged by waves.
  • Idea 2: Volunteers placed barriers around the plantings.
  • Relationship: The risk explains the action.
  • Transition: For this reason
  • Final sentence: Young mangrove trees can be damaged by waves. For this reason, volunteers placed barriers around the plantings.
Common mistake: For example would make the closing of the entrance a sample of flooding, not an effect of flooding.
Correct logic: As a result fits because the closing happened because of the flood.
Takeaway: Cause/effect = because A, B happens; do not confuse a result with an example.
Practice 4.1
Easy

The river's water level dropped sharply during the dry season. ______ several ferry routes had to be suspended until rainfall returned.

A. As a result,
B. For example,
C. Earlier,
D. Nevertheless,
Practice 4.2
Hard

The parasitic European cuckoo decreases the mother bird's output and, in some cases, kills all offspring, ______ decreasing the host bird's fitness. On top of reproductive output, the mother bird must put in additional work to feed the equivalent of a nest full of babies that is just one large baby.

A. however
B. but
C. therefore
D. previously

5. Time, Restatement, and Summary

  • Time transitions need real sequence clues: first, next, later, earlier, dates, or steps.
  • Restatement transitions mean idea 2 says the same idea again in clearer words.
  • Summary transitions give a broad final takeaway from several earlier details.
  • Do not use in conclusion for a new detail or next step.
  • Do not use in other words unless idea 2 truly restates or clarifies idea 1.
Recognition cue: Use time transitions only when the sentence gives dates, steps, or order. Use in other words or that is only when the second idea restates or clarifies the first.

How It Looks In A Question

Mini demonstration 1: Date-based sequence
  • Idea 1: The club observed the comet on Tuesday.
  • Idea 2: Members photographed it again on Friday.
  • Relationship: The second event happens later.
  • Transition: Later
  • Final sentence: The club observed the comet on Tuesday. Later, members photographed it again on Friday.
Mini demonstration 2: Definition to plain explanation
  • Idea 1: A spectrometer separates light into wavelengths.
  • Idea 2: It helps identify materials by light patterns.
  • Relationship: Idea 2 explains what idea 1 means in use.
  • Transition: In other words
  • Final sentence: A spectrometer separates light into wavelengths; in other words, it helps identify materials by light patterns.
Common mistake: In conclusion would signal a final summary, but the sentence simply moves to a later step.
Correct logic: Later fits because the second event happens after the first event.
Takeaway: Time needs sequence clues; restatement means same idea again; summary means a broad final takeaway.
Practice 5.1
Easy

The astronomy club first observed the comet just before sunrise on Tuesday. ______ members photographed it again on Friday, when it appeared slightly brighter.

A. Later,
B. In other words,
C. On the contrary,
D. Therefore,
Practice 5.2
Medium

The instrument is a spectrometer, a device that separates light into its component wavelengths; ______ it allows researchers to identify materials by the patterns of light they absorb.

A. meanwhile,
B. in other words,
C. nevertheless,
D. as a result,

If I could only offer you one tip for the future, that would be it." ______ begins a well-known commencement speech; the speaker goes on to explain why his advice about careers and life goals may or may not be helpful to the students, but the advice to wear sunscreen will always be applicable.

One-Minute Checklist

  1. Shorten the text into idea 1 and idea 2.
  2. Circle anchor words: dates, but/although, cause words, examples, numbers, definitions.
  3. Name the relationship before reading the choices.
  4. Eliminate transitions with the wrong function.
  5. Plug in the remaining choice and reread the two ideas.
