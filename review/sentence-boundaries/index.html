Sentence Boundaries

Build the clause skeleton first. Then choose punctuation by structure—not by pause, length, or appearance.

Review-only reference Every knowledge point includes a resolved demonstration and, when useful, its closest trap. This handout contains no exercises or unanswered items.

Part 1

Build the sentence foundation

Punctuation decisions begin with subjects, finite verbs, and complete thoughts.

1

A complete sentence needs a working skeleton

An independent clause has a subject, a finite verb, and a complete thought. Objects and complements may be required to finish the meaning of the verb.

Resolved demonstration The research team confirmed the result. subject + finite verb + object = IC
Closest trapThe research team confirming the result. The -ing form does not supply a finite main verb, so this is a fragment.
2

Finite verbs carry the clause

A finite verb shows tense or works with a finite helping verb. An -ing form, a to-verb, or a past participle cannot carry a clause by itself.

Resolved demonstration The data were collected in May and revealed a steady decline. were = finite helper; collected = participle; revealed = second finite verb
Closest trapThe data collected in May. Here, collected only describes data; no finite verb completes the sentence.
3

Independent clause, dependent clause, and phrase are different

An independent clause can stand alone. A dependent clause has a subject and verb but begins with a dependency word. A phrase lacks a complete subject–finite-verb structure.

Resolved demonstrations
IC: The glacier retreated.
DC: Because the glacier retreated
Phrase: After the long winter
Dependency signalsCommon signals include although, because, if, since, when, while, unless, whereas, who, which, and that.
4

Long subjects do not create new boundaries

Prepositional phrases and modifiers inside a subject do not change the core subject–verb connection. Locate the head subject and its finite verb.

Resolved demonstration The collection of nineteenth-century maps belongs to the university. complete subject | finite verb; no punctuation
Closest trapThe collection of nineteenth-century maps, belongs to the university. The comma illegally splits the subject from its verb.
5

One subject can control two verbs

A compound predicate is still one clause. Do not treat the second verb as a new independent clause unless it has its own subject.

Resolved comparison
One clause: The team measured the samples and recorded the results.
Two clauses: The team measured the samples, and the assistant recorded the results.
one subject + two verbs = no comma | two subjects + two finite verbs = comma + and

Part 2

Connect clauses legally

First classify both sides. Then use the small set of structures that Standard English permits.

6

Two independent clauses need a strong join

FANBOYS is the memory acronym for the seven coordinating conjunctions: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. When one of these words joins two independent clauses, use a comma before it. A period or semicolon can also make the boundary legal.

Three legal versions
The first trial failed. The team revised the method.
The first trial failed; the team revised the method.
The first trial failed, so the team revised the method.
IC . IC | IC ; IC | IC , FANBOYS IC
Illegal versionsThe first trial failed, the team revised the method. is a comma splice. The first trial failed the team revised the method. is a fused sentence.
7

A conjunctive adverb is not FANBOYS

Words such as however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, consequently, and instead cannot join independent clauses with commas alone.

Resolved demonstration The first trial failed; however, the team revised the method. IC ; transition, IC
Also legalThe first trial failed. However, the team revised the method.
Closest trapThe first trial failed, however, the team revised the method. still contains a comma splice.
8

An initial dependent unit attaches with a comma

When a dependent clause or introductory phrase comes before the main clause, place a comma at the end of that opening unit.

Resolved demonstrations
Although the first trial failed, the team revised the method.
After reviewing the data, the team revised the method.
DC, IC | introductory phrase, IC
Closest trapA period or semicolon after the initial dependent clause would leave a fragment.
9

An essential final dependent clause usually stays attached

A dependent clause that follows and completes the main idea normally takes no comma when it states an essential reason, condition, time, or identity.

Resolved comparison
Essential reason: The team repeated the trial because the sensor had failed.
Added contrast: The team repeated the trial, although the initial result remained plausible.
Meaning controls punctuationThe first final clause is required to explain why the trial was repeated. The second adds a contrasting comment and may be set off.

Part 3

Separate identifying information from extra information

Length does not decide. Meaning and sentence structure decide.

10

Specifying information identifies; extra information comments

Specifying information is needed to identify the noun and receives no surrounding punctuation. Extra information can be removed without changing the intended reference and receives punctuation.

Resolved comparison
Specifying: The sensor that failed during launch was replaced.
Extra: The primary sensor, which failed during launch, was replaced.
needed identity = no pair | removable comment = matched pair
Useful language cueThat commonly begins specifying information. Which commonly begins extra information and therefore appears with punctuation.
11

Extra information needs matched boundaries

Commas, dashes, or parentheses may enclose a removable interruption. Use one matching system around the whole unit.

Resolved demonstrations
Maya Chen, the project director, presented the findings.
Maya Chen—the project director—presented the findings.
Maya Chen (the project director) presented the findings.
Closest trapMaya Chen, the project director—presented the findings. mixes boundary systems and leaves the interruption unmatched.
12

Names and titles follow the same meaning test

A name is not automatically surrounded by commas. Decide whether it identifies the person or merely renames someone already identified.

Resolved comparison
Name identifies: Biologist Rachel Carson published the book.
Name already identified: Rachel Carson, a marine biologist, published the book.
Closest trapPunctuating every title or name as extra information without testing whether the noun remains specifically identified.

Part 4

Know each mark's permission and limit

A punctuation mark is correct only when the structure licenses its exact job.

13

Comma permissions are limited

On SAT Boundaries items, commas most often mark an introductory unit, separate simple list items, enclose extra information, or appear before FANBOYS joining two independent clauses.

Four resolved jobs
Introductory unit: After sunset, the temperature fell.
List: The kit contained water, food, and a flashlight.
Extra information: The sample, collected in May, remained stable.
IC join: The temperature fell, so the lake froze.
Never use pause aloneA spoken pause does not prove a comma. Clause status and grammatical function control the mark.
14

A colon or single dash needs a complete lead-in

A colon or single dash may introduce an explanation, example, restatement, or list only after a complete clause.

Resolved demonstration The delay had one cause: a damaged cable. complete lead-in : explanation
Equivalent emphasisThe delay had one cause—a damaged cable. A single dash can perform the same completion job with stronger emphasis.
Closest trapThe cause was: a damaged cable. The colon illegally separates the linking verb from its complement.
15

Lists require visible item boundaries

Use commas between simple parallel items. Use semicolons between complex items that already contain commas. A colon introduces a list only after a complete lead-in.

Resolved demonstrations
Simple: We packed maps, water, and food.
Complex: The speakers were Ana Cruz, a chemist; David Li, an engineer; and Mina Shah, a physician.
Parallel-item checkEach list item must perform the same grammatical job. Punctuation cannot repair a list whose items are not parallel.
16

No punctuation is a structural choice

Do not split a required grammatical unit. A blank or answer choice does not prove that a mark belongs there.

Resolved no-mark units
Subject–verb: The final decision surprised the audience.
Verb–object: The committee approved the proposal.
Preposition–object: The pages from the archive were restored.
Adjective–noun: The newly restored manuscript is on display.
Compound predicate: The team collected the samples and analyzed them.
Specifying modifier: The manuscript that contains the map is on display.

Part 5

Clause-Skeleton Punctuation

Use one stable method for every Sentence Boundaries item.

1

Read the whole sentence

Meaning helps reveal where one grammatical unit ends and another begins.

2

Mark subjects and finite verbs

Ignore descriptive phrases until the core skeleton is visible.

3

Classify both sides

Label each side IC, DC, phrase, extra information, list item, or required unit.

4

Name the boundary job

State the exact job: join, attach, enclose, introduce, separate, or preserve.

5

Choose a licensed pattern

Match the structure to the boundary map. Do not choose by sound or visual balance.

6

Audit the full sentence

Check for a fragment, comma splice, fused sentence, unmatched pair, or split unit.

Full-method demonstration · solve the boundaries first

Say each boundary mark before clicking its covered slot. Click the same slot again to hide it.

Because the archive had been damaged the conservators photographed each page however they postponed restoration.
Skeleton: Because the archive had been damaged = DC. The conservators photographed each page = IC. They postponed restoration = IC.
First boundary: Initial DC attaches to the first IC with a comma.
Second boundary: Two ICs are linked by however, so the legal pattern is semicolon + transition + comma.
Audit: No fragment, comma splice, fused sentence, unmatched interruption, or split required unit remains.

Part 6

High-frequency trap library

Each trap corrupts one field in the method.

Pause trap

Sound replaces structure.

RepairThe unusually long migration route crosses the desert. Long subject, no subject–verb comma.

False-verb trap

An -ing, to-verb, or participle is mistaken for a finite verb.

RepairThe researchers examining the sample noticed a crack. Noticed carries the clause.

Comma-splice trap

A comma alone is used between two independent clauses.

RepairThe sample was stable; the team continued the test.

Conjunction-only trap

FANBOYS appears between two ICs without its comma.

RepairThe sample was stable, and the team continued the test.

Semicolon-fragment trap

A semicolon is placed before a DC or phrase.

RepairThe team continued the test because the sample was stable. No semicolon before the essential reason clause.

Colon-by-list trap

A list is noticed, but the lead-in is unfinished.

RepairThe kit contained water, food, and a flashlight. No colon after the verb contained.

One-sided-pair trap

Only one side of extra information is marked.

RepairThe sample, collected in May, remained stable.

Overpunctuation trap

A mark splits one required unit.

RepairThe pages from the archive were restored. Keep the preposition with its object.

False-new-clause trap

A second verb under the same subject is treated as a new IC.

RepairThe team photographed the pages and postponed restoration.

List-edge trap

An internal comma is mistaken for the end of a complex list item.

RepairThe panel included Ana Cruz, a chemist; David Li, an engineer; and Mina Shah, a physician.

Part 7

One-page recall card

No new content—only the complete decision system in compact form.

SkeletonFind subjects and finite verbs. Treat modifiers as removable until the core is visible.
StatusClassify each side as IC, DC, phrase, extra information, list item, or required unit.
Two ICsUse period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS. Never comma alone.
Transition between ICsUse IC; however, IC or start a new sentence.
Dependent openingUse DC, IC. Keep an essential final DC attached.
Extra informationRemove it, verify the same reference remains, then use a matched pair.
Colon or single dashRequire a complete lead-in before an explanation, example, restatement, or list.
ListsBracket full parallel items. Use commas for simple items and semicolons for complex items.
No punctuationPreserve subject–verb, verb–object, preposition–object, adjective–noun, compound predicates, and specifying modifiers.
Final auditReject fragments, comma splices, fused sentences, unmatched pairs, and split required units.
